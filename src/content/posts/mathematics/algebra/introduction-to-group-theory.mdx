Group theory is the mathematical study of **symmetry and structure**. Instead of focusing on what particular objects are made of, group theory studies the operations that can be performed on them and the rules those operations obey.

The same abstract structure appears in many different places:

- adding integers,
- rotating a polygon,
- permuting a collection of objects,
- multiplying invertible matrices,
- and studying symmetries of equations.

Once we recognize that these examples satisfy the same axioms, we can prove results about all of them at once.

## What is a group?

A **group** is a set $G$ together with an operation

$$
\ast \colon G \times G \longrightarrow G
$$

that combines two elements $a,b\in G$ to produce another element $a\ast b\in G$.

The pair $(G,\ast)$ is a group when the following four properties hold.

### 1. Closure

For every $a,b\in G$,

$$
a\ast b\in G.
$$

The operation never takes us outside the set.

### 2. Associativity

For every $a,b,c\in G$,

$$
(a\ast b)\ast c=a\ast(b\ast c).
$$

Associativity allows us to write $a\ast b\ast c$ without ambiguity. Notice that associativity is not the same as commutativity: it changes the placement of parentheses, not the order of the elements.

### 3. Identity element

There exists an element $e\in G$ such that, for every $a\in G$,

$$
e\ast a=a\ast e=a.
$$

The element $e$ is called the **identity** of the group.

### 4. Inverses

For every $a\in G$, there exists an element $a^{-1}\in G$ such that

$$
a\ast a^{-1}=a^{-1}\ast a=e.
$$

The element $a^{-1}$ is called the **inverse** of $a$.

We often suppress the operation symbol and write

$$
ab
$$

instead of $a\ast b$. In this notation, the group axioms become

$$
(ab)c=a(bc),\qquad ea=ae=a,\qquad aa^{-1}=a^{-1}a=e.
$$

## The integers under addition

The most familiar example of a group is

$$
(\mathbb Z,+).
$$

Let $a,b,c\in\mathbb Z$. Then:

$$
a+b\in\mathbb Z,
$$

$$
(a+b)+c=a+(b+c),
$$

$$
0+a=a+0=a,
$$

and

$$
a+(-a)=(-a)+a=0.
$$

Therefore $(\mathbb Z,+)$ is a group. Its identity is $0$, and the inverse of $a$ is $-a$.

Because the operation is addition, we usually write the inverse as $-a$ rather than $a^{-1}$. Similarly, repeated addition is written

$$
na=\underbrace{a+a+\cdots+a}_{n\text{ times}}
$$

for $n>0$.

## A non-example: the integers under multiplication

Consider $(\mathbb Z,\cdot)$. Multiplication is closed and associative, and $1$ is an identity:

$$
1\cdot a=a\cdot1=a.
$$

However, most integers do not have multiplicative inverses in $\mathbb Z$. For example, an inverse of $2$ would have to satisfy

$$
2x=1,
$$

so $x=\frac12$, but $\frac12\notin\mathbb Z$. Therefore

$$
(\mathbb Z,\cdot)
$$

is not a group.

If we instead use the nonzero rational numbers,

$$
\mathbb Q^\times=\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\},
$$

then $(\mathbb Q^\times,\cdot)$ is a group because every $q\neq0$ has the inverse

$$
q^{-1}=\frac1q.
$$

We exclude $0$ because no number $x$ can satisfy $0x=1$.

## Abelian and non-abelian groups

A group $(G,\ast)$ is **abelian** if

$$
a\ast b=b\ast a
$$

for all $a,b\in G$.

The groups $(\mathbb Z,+)$ and $(\mathbb Q^\times,\cdot)$ are abelian. Not every group is.

For example, rotations in three-dimensional space generally do not commute. Rotating an object $90^\circ$ around the $x$-axis and then $90^\circ$ around the $y$-axis usually produces a different result from performing the rotations in the opposite order:

$$
R_xR_y\neq R_yR_x.
$$

The order of operations matters in a non-abelian group.

## Basic consequences of the axioms

The group axioms are short, but they imply many useful facts.

### The identity is unique

Suppose $e$ and $f$ are both identity elements. Since $e$ is an identity,

$$
ef=f.
$$

Since $f$ is an identity,

$$
ef=e.
$$

Therefore

$$
e=f.
$$

A group cannot have two different identity elements.

### Every inverse is unique

Suppose $b$ and $c$ are both inverses of $a$. Then

$$
ab=ba=e
$$

and

$$
ac=ca=e.
$$

Using associativity,

$$
b=be=b(ac)=(ba)c=ec=c.
$$

Thus the inverse of $a$ is unique.

### The inverse of a product

For $a,b\in G$,

$$
(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}.
$$

The order reverses because

$$
(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1})
=a(bb^{-1})a^{-1}
=aea^{-1}
=aa^{-1}
=e,
$$

and similarly,

$$
(b^{-1}a^{-1})(ab)=e.
$$

This is analogous to putting on socks and then shoes: to undo the process, we remove the shoes first and the socks second.

### Cancellation

If

$$
ab=ac,
$$

then multiplying both sides on the left by $a^{-1}$ gives

$$
a^{-1}(ab)=a^{-1}(ac).
$$

Associativity now gives

$$
(a^{-1}a)b=(a^{-1}a)c,
$$

so

$$
eb=ec
$$

and therefore

$$
b=c.
$$

This is the **left cancellation law**. Similarly,

$$
ba=ca\implies b=c.
$$

## Finite groups and Cayley tables

A group is **finite** if it contains finitely many elements. The number of elements in $G$ is called the **order** of $G$ and is denoted by

$$
|G|.
$$

For a finite group, its operation can be displayed in a **Cayley table**.

Consider the integers modulo $3$ under addition:

$$
\mathbb Z_3=\{0,1,2\}.
$$

Addition is performed modulo $3$, so

$$
1+2\equiv0\pmod3
$$

and

$$
2+2\equiv1\pmod3.
$$

Its Cayley table is

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
+_3 & 0 & 1 & 2\\
\hline
0 & 0 & 1 & 2\\
1 & 1 & 2 & 0\\
2 & 2 & 0 & 1
\end{array}
$$

Every row and every column contains each group element exactly once. This follows from cancellation. For example, if two entries in the row corresponding to $a$ were equal, then

$$
ab=ac
$$

would imply $b=c$.

## Subgroups

A subset $H\subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** of $G$ if $H$ is itself a group under the operation inherited from $G$. We write

$$
H\leq G.
$$

Every group has at least two obvious subgroups:

$$
\{e\}\leq G
$$

and

$$
G\leq G.
$$

The first is called the **trivial subgroup**.

For example, the even integers form a subgroup of $(\mathbb Z,+)$:

$$
2\mathbb Z=\{2k:k\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

Indeed, if $2m,2n\in2\mathbb Z$, then

$$
2m+2n=2(m+n)\in2\mathbb Z,
$$

and

$$
-(2m)=2(-m)\in2\mathbb Z.
$$

The identity $0=2\cdot0$ also belongs to $2\mathbb Z$.

### The subgroup test

A nonempty subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup if, for every $a,b\in H$,

$$
ab^{-1}\in H.
$$

This single condition contains everything we need.

Taking $a=b$ gives

$$
aa^{-1}=e\in H.
$$

Now that $e\in H$, taking $a=e$ gives

$$
eb^{-1}=b^{-1}\in H.
$$

Finally, since $b^{-1}\in H$, applying the condition to $a$ and $b^{-1}$ gives

$$
a(b^{-1})^{-1}=ab\in H.
$$

Thus $H$ contains the identity, inverses, and products.

## Cyclic groups

Let $a\in G$. The subgroup generated by $a$ is

$$
\langle a\rangle=\{a^n:n\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

Here,

$$
a^0=e,\qquad a^{-n}=(a^{-1})^n.
$$

If

$$
G=\langle a\rangle,
$$

then $G$ is called a **cyclic group**, and $a$ is called a **generator** of $G$.

The additive group of integers is cyclic:

$$
\mathbb Z=\langle1\rangle=\langle-1\rangle.
$$

In additive notation,

$$
\langle a\rangle=\{na:n\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

The group $\mathbb Z_6$ is also cyclic:

$$
\mathbb Z_6=\langle1\rangle
=\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}.
$$

Starting from $1$ and repeatedly adding $1$ modulo $6$ gives

$$
0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 0,\ldots
$$

The element $5$ is another generator because

$$
\langle5\rangle
=\{0,5,4,3,2,1\}
=\mathbb Z_6.
$$

However,

$$
\langle2\rangle=\{0,2,4\}
$$

is only a proper subgroup of $\mathbb Z_6$.

Every cyclic group is abelian. If $x=a^m$ and $y=a^n$, then

$$
xy=a^ma^n=a^{m+n}=a^{n+m}=a^na^m=yx.
$$

## The order of an element

The **order** of an element $a\in G$, written $\operatorname{ord}(a)$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that

$$
a^n=e.
$$

If no such positive integer exists, then $a$ has infinite order.

In $\mathbb Z_6$, using additive notation, the order of $2$ is $3$ because

$$
2+2+2\equiv0\pmod6,
$$

while neither $2$ nor $2+2$ is congruent to $0$ modulo $6$.

Thus

$$
\operatorname{ord}(2)=3.
$$

More generally, in the additive group $\mathbb Z_n$,

$$
\operatorname{ord}(k)=\frac{n}{\gcd(n,k)}.
$$

For example,

$$
\operatorname{ord}(8\text{ in }\mathbb Z_{12})
=\frac{12}{\gcd(12,8)}
=\frac{12}{4}
=3.
$$

Indeed,

$$
8,\quad 8+8\equiv4,\quad 8+8+8\equiv0\pmod{12}.
$$

## Permutation groups

A **permutation** of a set is a bijection from the set to itself. The set of all permutations of

$$
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
$$

forms the **symmetric group** $S_n$ under composition.

The group $S_3$ has

$$
|S_3|=3!=6
$$

elements.

One permutation is

$$
\sigma=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&3\\
2&3&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This means

$$
\sigma(1)=2,\qquad
\sigma(2)=3,\qquad
\sigma(3)=1.
$$

In cycle notation,

$$
\sigma=(1\ 2\ 3).
$$

Applying $\sigma$ three times returns every element to its starting position:

$$
\sigma^3=e.
$$

Therefore

$$
\operatorname{ord}(\sigma)=3.
$$

Permutation composition is generally not commutative. Let

$$
\alpha=(1\ 2),\qquad \beta=(2\ 3).
$$

Using the convention that the rightmost permutation acts first,

$$
\alpha\beta=(1\ 2)(2\ 3)=(1\ 2\ 3),
$$

whereas

$$
\beta\alpha=(2\ 3)(1\ 2)=(1\ 3\ 2).
$$

Hence

$$
\alpha\beta\neq\beta\alpha,
$$

so $S_3$ is non-abelian.

## Symmetry groups

Group theory gives a precise language for symmetry.

Consider an equilateral triangle. The transformations that leave it looking unchanged are:

- the identity transformation,
- rotations by $120^\circ$ and $240^\circ$,
- and three reflections.

These six symmetries form the **dihedral group**

$$
D_3.
$$

Let $r$ denote rotation by $120^\circ$ and let $s$ denote a reflection. Then

$$
r^3=e,\qquad s^2=e,
$$

and the interaction between rotation and reflection is described by

$$
srs=r^{-1}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
sr=r^{-1}s.
$$

The group can be written as

$$
D_3=\{e,r,r^2,s,sr,sr^2\}.
$$

Since

$$
sr\neq rs,
$$

the group $D_3$ is non-abelian. In fact,

$$
D_3\cong S_3.
$$

The symbol $\cong$ means that the two groups have the same abstract group structure even though their elements are described differently.

## Homomorphisms

A **group homomorphism** is a function

$$
\varphi\colon G\longrightarrow H
$$

that preserves the group operation:

$$
\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)
$$

for all $a,b\in G$.

If the groups use additive notation, this becomes

$$
\varphi(a+b)=\varphi(a)+\varphi(b).
$$

For example, define

$$
\varphi\colon\mathbb Z\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_n
$$

by

$$
\varphi(k)=[k]_n.
$$

Then

$$
\varphi(a+b)
=[a+b]_n
=[a]_n+[b]_n
=\varphi(a)+\varphi(b),
$$

so $\varphi$ is a homomorphism.

Every homomorphism sends the identity to the identity:

$$
\varphi(e_G)=e_H.
$$

To see this, observe that

$$
\varphi(e_G)
=\varphi(e_Ge_G)
=\varphi(e_G)\varphi(e_G).
$$

Cancellation in $H$ gives

$$
\varphi(e_G)=e_H.
$$

Homomorphisms also preserve inverses:

$$
\varphi(a^{-1})=\varphi(a)^{-1}.
$$

Indeed,

$$
\varphi(a)\varphi(a^{-1})
=\varphi(aa^{-1})
=\varphi(e_G)
=e_H.
$$

## Kernel and image

For a homomorphism $\varphi\colon G\to H$, its **kernel** is

$$
\ker\varphi
=\{g\in G:\varphi(g)=e_H\},
$$

and its **image** is

$$
\operatorname{im}\varphi
=\{\varphi(g):g\in G\}.
$$

For the reduction map

$$
\varphi\colon\mathbb Z\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_n,
\qquad
\varphi(k)=[k]_n,
$$

we have

$$
\ker\varphi=n\mathbb Z
$$

because

$$
[k]_n=[0]_n
\iff n\mid k.
$$

Also,

$$
\operatorname{im}\varphi=\mathbb Z_n
$$

because every congruence class $[r]_n$ is the image of the integer $r$.

The kernel measures which elements become indistinguishable under the homomorphism. A homomorphism is injective exactly when

$$
\ker\varphi=\{e_G\}.
$$

## Cosets

Let $H\leq G$ and $g\in G$. The **left coset** of $H$ determined by $g$ is

$$
gH=\{gh:h\in H\}.
$$

The corresponding right coset is

$$
Hg=\{hg:h\in H\}.
$$

In an additive group, we write

$$
g+H=\{g+h:h\in H\}.
$$

Consider the subgroup

$$
H=3\mathbb Z
$$

of $\mathbb Z$. Its cosets are

$$
0+H=3\mathbb Z,
$$

$$
1+H=\{3k+1:k\in\mathbb Z\},
$$

and

$$
2+H=\{3k+2:k\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

Every integer belongs to exactly one of these three cosets. They are precisely the three congruence classes modulo $3$.

Every coset $gH$ has the same number of elements as $H$ because the function

$$
H\longrightarrow gH,\qquad h\longmapsto gh
$$

is a bijection.

## Lagrange's theorem

One of the first major results in finite group theory is **Lagrange's theorem**:

> If $G$ is a finite group and $H\leq G$, then
>
> $$
> |H|\mid|G|.
> $$

In other words, the order of every subgroup divides the order of the group.

The reason is that the left cosets of $H$ partition $G$, and every coset contains exactly $|H|$ elements. If there are $[G:H]$ distinct cosets, then

$$
|G|=[G:H]|H|.
$$

The number $[G:H]$ is called the **index** of $H$ in $G$.

As an immediate consequence, the order of every element divides the order of the group:

$$
\operatorname{ord}(a)=|\langle a\rangle|\mid|G|.
$$

For example, a group of order $12$ cannot contain an element of order $5$, because

$$
5\nmid12.
$$

If $|G|=p$ for a prime number $p$, then every non-identity element has order $p$. Therefore every group of prime order is cyclic:

$$
|G|=p\implies G\cong\mathbb Z_p.
$$

## Normal subgroups and quotient groups

A subgroup $N\leq G$ is **normal** if

$$
gN=Ng
$$

for every $g\in G$. We write

$$
N\trianglelefteq G.
$$

An equivalent condition is

$$
gNg^{-1}=N
$$

for every $g\in G$.

Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal because

$$
gng^{-1}
=ngg^{-1}
=n
$$

for all $g\in G$ and $n\in N$.

When $N\trianglelefteq G$, the set of cosets

$$
G/N=\{gN:g\in G\}
$$

becomes a group under

$$
(gN)(hN)=(gh)N.
$$

For example,

$$
\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z
$$

is the quotient of $\mathbb Z$ by the subgroup $n\mathbb Z$. Its elements are

$$
[0]_n,[1]_n,\ldots,[n-1]_n.
$$

This connects modular arithmetic directly with group theory.

## A compact example

Consider the additive group

$$
G=\mathbb Z_{12}.
$$

Let

$$
H=\langle4\rangle.
$$

Repeatedly adding $4$ gives

$$
0,\ 4,\ 8,\ 0,
$$

so

$$
H=\{0,4,8\}
$$

and

$$
|H|=3.
$$

The distinct cosets of $H$ are

$$
\begin{aligned}
0+H&=\{0,4,8\},\\
1+H&=\{1,5,9\},\\
2+H&=\{2,6,10\},\\
3+H&=\{3,7,11\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
[G:H]=4
$$

and Lagrange's theorem appears concretely as

$$
|G|=[G:H]|H|=4\cdot3=12.
$$

Because $G$ is abelian, $H$ is normal, and the quotient group has order

$$
|G/H|=4.
$$

In fact,

$$
\mathbb Z_{12}/\langle4\rangle\cong\mathbb Z_4.
$$

## Exercises

### Exercise 1

Show that

$$
3\mathbb Z=\{3k:k\in\mathbb Z\}
$$

is a subgroup of $(\mathbb Z,+)$.

### Exercise 2

Find every generator of $\mathbb Z_{10}$.

### Exercise 3

Calculate the order of every element of $\mathbb Z_8$.

### Exercise 4

Let

$$
\varphi\colon\mathbb Z\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_6,
\qquad
\varphi(k)=[k]_6.
$$

Find $\ker\varphi$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi$.

### Exercise 5

Let $G$ be a finite group with $|G|=15$. What element orders are permitted by Lagrange's theorem?

## Answers

For Exercise 1, the set is nonempty, and for $3m,3n\in3\mathbb Z$,

$$
3m-3n=3(m-n)\in3\mathbb Z.
$$

Therefore the subgroup test gives

$$
3\mathbb Z\leq\mathbb Z.
$$

For Exercise 2, an element $k\in\mathbb Z_{10}$ generates the group exactly when

$$
\gcd(k,10)=1.
$$

Hence the generators are

$$
1,\ 3,\ 7,\ 9.
$$

For Exercise 3, use

$$
\operatorname{ord}(k)=\frac8{\gcd(8,k)}.
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{array}{c|cccccccc}
k&0&1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\
\hline
\operatorname{ord}(k)&1&8&4&8&2&8&4&8
\end{array}
$$

For Exercise 4,

$$
\ker\varphi=6\mathbb Z
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{im}\varphi=\mathbb Z_6.
$$

For Exercise 5, the order of an element must divide $15$. Therefore the only possibilities are

$$
1,\ 3,\ 5,\ 15.
$$

Lagrange's theorem permits these orders, although it does not by itself guarantee that elements of every permitted order exist.

## Where to go next

The basic objects of group theory now fit into one picture:

$$
\text{elements}
\longrightarrow
\text{subgroups}
\longrightarrow
\text{cosets}
\longrightarrow
\text{quotient groups}.
$$

At the same time, homomorphisms compare different groups:

$$
\varphi\colon G\longrightarrow H.
$$

The next natural topics are:

- group actions,
- the isomorphism theorems,
- direct products,
- the classification of finite abelian groups,
- Sylow's theorems,
- and presentations by generators and relations.

The main habit to develop is to move constantly between abstract axioms and concrete examples. Groups become intuitive when we see the same structure in integers, permutations, matrices, and geometric symmetries.
